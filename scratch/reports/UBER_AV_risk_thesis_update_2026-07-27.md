# UBER autonomous-vehicle risk: thesis and KPI update

**As of:** July 27, 2026
**Decision use:** AV-specific overlay for position sizing; not a full refresh of every Uber risk
**Decision status:** Owner approved July 27, 2026
**Current portfolio weight:** 2.37%
**Recommended AV stance:** Hold at roughly 2.0%-2.5%; cap at 3.0% until AV economics and same-market competitive effects are disclosed.

## Bottom line

Waymo is a real, scaled structural competitor today. Tesla is a credible second threat, but its public evidence is still far behind Waymo on commercial scale, normalized safety, fleet size, trip volume, and unit economics. Uber's hybrid-network argument is strategically coherent: fragmented AV supply should value Uber's demand, routing, support, and fleet-management layer. The missing evidence is whether that role earns an attractive, capital-light take rate once the best AV suppliers have their own consumer apps.

The key risk is therefore not "AV replaces Uber." It is that Waymo and Tesla become a concentrated supplier duopoly, distribute directly, and force Uber either to accept a much lower take rate or fund vehicles, guarantees, depots, and incentives. That would weaken both the marketplace margin and the asset-light free-cash-flow thesis.

At the current price of $65.94, approximately $136.6 billion of equity value, and about $9.8 billion of trailing free cash flow, Uber trades at roughly 13.9x trailing FCF, or a 7.2% FCF yield. A simple equity DCF implies the market is pricing:

- about 1.7% five-year FCF growth at a 10% cost of equity and 3% terminal growth;
- about 6.0% growth at an 11% cost of equity and 2.5% terminal growth; or
- about 9.5% growth if normalized starting FCF is reduced to $8.5 billion under the latter assumptions.

This is already a meaningful deceleration discount. It is not a full disintermediation price: a durable no-growth outcome is worth roughly $61 per share under the 10%/3% case, while 5% growth is about $76 and 10% growth is about $93. The market appears to price moderate long-term pressure plus other Uber risks, not a collapse of the network.

## Independent competitive assessment

### Waymo: high-confidence threat

Waymo is no longer a technology demonstration:

- Alphabet disclosed more than 500,000 fully autonomous rides per week across 11 major U.S. cities in Q1 2026.
- Waymo reports more than 4 million fully autonomous miles per week and 220.6 million rider-only miles through March 2026.
- Its exposure-normalized safety analysis reports 94% fewer serious-injury-or-worse crashes and 82% fewer injury-causing crashes than matched human benchmarks. The methodology is imperfect, but it is substantially more decision-useful than raw crash counts.
- Waymo raised $16 billion at a $126 billion post-money valuation and plans operations in more than 20 additional cities in 2026.
- Waymo distributes through Uber only in Austin and Atlanta. It uses its own app in other markets and has selected Avis, rather than Uber, for end-to-end Dallas fleet management.

This evidence disproves the view that AV competition is distant. Waymo's current weekly rides are still less than 1% of Uber's roughly 280 million weekly platform trips, so the near-term consolidated financial effect should be small. Its importance is as proof that a direct, vertically coordinated AV network can achieve commercial scale and as a constraint on Uber's terminal economics.

### Tesla: credible option, not yet Waymo-equivalent evidence

Tesla has moved beyond a purely promised product:

- Its Q2 2026 deck shows paid Robotaxi operations and a cumulative-paid-miles chart of roughly 2.5 million miles.
- Tesla's consumer page currently names autonomous rides in Austin, Dallas, Houston, and Miami; the Q2 deck describes a broader operating or preparation footprint, while the Bay Area still uses supervised driving.
- Active FSD subscriptions reached 1.48 million, providing a large software and data-distribution base.
- Cybercab production has begun, but July activity described in the deck was engineering drives and employee rides rather than a disclosed scaled public fleet.

The case is not yet proven at Waymo's evidentiary standard:

- Tesla does not disclose Robotaxi fleet size, paid trips, rides per vehicle, contribution profit, remote-assistance rates, or exposure-normalized safety.
- California lists Tesla only for testing with a driver, not driverless testing or AV deployment.
- Tesla expects more than $25 billion of 2026 capital expenditure and acknowledges dedicated cleaning, maintenance, charging, security, teleoperations, and fleet-management infrastructure. Its economic advantage cannot be inferred from eliminating the human driver alone.
- Tesla's 10-Q says regulators including NHTSA, NTSB, the SEC, and DOJ have ongoing information requests involving vehicle incidents, FSD, and Robotaxi; it also says no agency has concluded wrongdoing.

Tesla is therefore a medium-confidence competitive threat with high upside to its own scale. Treat Tesla's direct app and manufacturing base as strategically important, but do not treat announced metros or installed Cybercab capacity as equivalent to a scaled, profitable rider-only network.

## AV scenario structure for the Uber thesis

These are explicit AV overlays, not standalone price targets.

| Scenario | Probability | Industry structure | Uber consequence |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **Fragmented supply / Uber demand layer wins** | 35% | Several scaled AV suppliers need a neutral demand and operations layer; consumers multi-home. | AV expands trip supply and lowers prices. Uber preserves an attractive platform take rate and remains capital-light. AV is neutral to positive for value. |
| **Mixed direct and aggregator distribution** | 45% | Waymo and Tesla distribute directly in dense metros but use Uber or peers selectively for utilization and market entry. | Uber retains broad demand but loses some premium urban volume and accepts lower AV economics. Apply a 10%-20% haircut to long-run Mobility profit versus the no-AV case. |
| **Concentrated direct AV networks** | 20% | Waymo and Tesla achieve large, reliable fleets and keep most demand in their own apps. Other suppliers fail to scale. | Uber loses high-value city trips or becomes a low-take-rate reseller and takes on capital commitments. Apply a 35%-50% long-run Mobility profit haircut and a lower multiple. |

The weighted conclusion is an approximately 15%-20% AV haircut to an otherwise unchanged Uber valuation. The current share price appears to absorb a meaningful portion of that haircut, but the uncertainty is high enough that AV should cap position size until the economic KPIs improve.

## Position-sizing rules

All thresholds below are **approved monitoring rules as of July 27, 2026**.

### Current action

Hold the current 2.37% position. Treat 2.0%-2.5% as the normal range and 3.0% as the pre-proof ceiling. Do not use the prior 3%-4% initial / 5%-6% add framework until the AV evidence gap closes.

### Add toward 3.5%-4.0%

Require at least two of the following, including at least one economic proof point:

1. Uber discloses absolute AV trips or active commercial AV fleet and an AV net-revenue, take-rate, or contribution-profit measure.
2. Two consecutive quarters show Uber Mobility growth in AV-overlap markets within 3 percentage points of comparable non-overlap markets, without material price or incentive deterioration.
3. Uber's disclosed AV investments, purchase commitments, guarantees, and fleet assets remain below 15% of trailing FCF, with contracted or demonstrated returns.
4. At least three independent AV suppliers each exceed 100 rider-only commercial vehicles on Uber, reducing dependence on Waymo.
5. AV rides per vehicle-day and gross bookings per vehicle-day are accretive to the network without more than 50 basis points of Mobility margin dilution.

### Trim toward 1.0%-1.5%

Any one of the following is sufficient:

1. Uber Mobility growth in AV-overlap cities trails matched control markets by more than 5 percentage points for two quarters.
2. Waymo ends Uber exclusivity in Austin or Atlanta, or shifts a material share of those cities to its direct app.
3. Mobility margin compresses more than 100 basis points year over year while AV volume rises and insurance is not the main cause.
4. Uber's AV capital at risk exceeds $2 billion or 20% of trailing FCF without disclosed contracted returns.
5. Disclosed AV platform take rate is below 10% and Uber must also fund utilization guarantees, vehicles, or depot economics.

### Exit or fully re-underwrite

1. U.S. Mobility trips or gross bookings decline for two quarters in multiple AV-heavy metros while direct Waymo or Tesla volumes scale.
2. Waymo and Tesla each reach at least 5%-10% of addressable rides in major overlap cities and Uber is excluded from most of those trips.
3. Uber becomes a material fleet owner or guarantor and AV contribution profit remains negative.
4. Regulatory approvals accelerate direct AV deployment while Uber's non-Waymo partners repeatedly miss commercial rider-only milestones.

## Questions that matter on Uber's August 5 call

1. What were absolute AV trips, active rider-only vehicles, and AV gross bookings in Q2, by partner or at least in aggregate?
2. What net revenue or contribution profit does Uber earn per AV trip versus a comparable human-driver trip?
3. How do Mobility trips, gross bookings, pricing, ETA, promotions, and category position in San Francisco, Los Angeles, Austin, and Atlanta compare with matched non-AV markets?
4. What AV equity investments, minimum purchase commitments, financing guarantees, residual-value risks, depot leases, or owned fleet assets are on or off balance sheet?
5. Are Austin and Atlanta still exclusive to the Uber app, and what prevents Waymo from shifting those cities to direct distribution?
6. How many of the 30-plus announced partners have more than 100 rider-only commercial vehicles or 10,000 paid trips?
7. What share of AV economics goes to the AV developer, vehicle owner/financier, fleet operator, insurer, and Uber?
